# Title hooks

A title hook is the headline that sits on screen for the first few seconds of a clip. It is read with the sound off, in about a second.

This is a round of its own, done after the clips are approved. It has three parts: write a lot of options, let the user shortlist, then put every shortlisted hook on its clip. Each hook becomes its own finished version of the clip, so they can test them against each other.

## 1. Write the options

If you have a hook-writing or title-writing skill, load it and follow it for the wording. This file only adds what is specific to clips.

- **Write from the finished clip, not from the script.** Transcribe each approved clip and write from those words. A hook must be paid off by what is actually said in that cut.
- **Ten options per clip.** All different ideas, not ten rewordings of one.
- **Lowercase.** "AI" is the one capital. Keep the host's own lines where they are strong. Seven or eight words at most.
- **Hook versions of the same clip get their own set.** If 08a to 08d each open on a different line, each gets ten of its own.

Put them in one document the user can read on their phone:

- A heading per clip: the clip number and a short plain name ("04 · The panic attack before my first workshop").
- The ten options as a numbered list, so they can answer with numbers.
- Two starred picks under each list, with one line on why each would make someone watch.
- Nothing else. No intro beyond two lines, no theory.

## 2. The user shortlists

They reply with numbers per clip, and may add lines of their own. Expect several per clip. On the first job the host chose 39 across 13 clips, between one and five each.

Take their list exactly. Do not swap in your starred picks. Do not rewrite a line they wrote.

### What tends to get picked

The first job's shortlist is a useful guide. Of 26 starred picks, the host took 7. What was chosen instead:

- **Plain statements of the point.** "AI is only as smart as you are". "AI is not a technical skill". "firing your team for AI is a mistake". "your first business isn't your last".
- **"why" and "how" lines.** "why AI can't give you an edge". "how to overcome stage fright". "why i quit my agency…".
- **The viewer's own words in quotes.** "AI is going to run my business". "can't AI just do my ads?".
- **A concrete promise.** "one winning ad, 500 variations". "1 strategist, 10x the output". "your team is wasting 2 hours a day".

What was passed over:

- Clever or literary lines. "nerves are just a weight you haven't lifted yet". "i wanted freedom. i built 10 bosses". "learn to drive, not the car".
- Cliffhangers that hide the topic. "i almost refunded 60 founders". "10 minutes before my first workshop…".
- Lines that lean on a detail from inside the clip, which a cold viewer cannot place.

The host's own additions were plainer still. When in doubt, write the line that says what the clip is about and who it is for.

Lean the next set of ten towards what the user picks. Still include a few bolder ones. Some get taken.

## 3. The look

The style that was approved: black text on a white rounded box, set in the platform's own "classic" text style (Instagram's default text). If the user already has a title style, use theirs and keep the placement and line-break rules below.

| | |
|---|---|
| Font | A heavy sans. In a phone editor, the platform's default "classic" text with a background. |
| Text colour | Near black, `#0A0C10` |
| Background | Solid white `#FFFFFF`, rounded box |
| Alignment | Centred |
| Position | Centred left to right. On the first job the middle of the box sat 56.6% of the way down the frame. |
| Timing | On from the first frame, for about 5 seconds |
| Animation | None |
| Casing | All lowercase, "AI" capitalised |
| Width | The box takes up no more than about three quarters of the frame's width |

The host's words on the reference: "black text with a white background and the placement on screen is below center so it can sit nicely in the frame."

### Where it sits, and why

On a close shot of the face, 56.6% down puts the box just under the chin, across the collar. The whole face is clear above it. The captions are clear below it, at 75% down.

That is the rule behind the number: **below the face, above the captions, with air on both sides.**

When the opening shot is framed differently, keep the rule and move the number:

- Find the chin and the top of the captions on a still of the first frame.
- Put the title box in the gap between them, nearer the chin.
- If the face sits lower in the frame and the gap is too small, move the title down only as far as needed and check it does not touch the captions. Note the change.
- On the first job no title had to move. All 39 sat at the same height, because every clip opens on the same close shot. Expect to move it only when the opening framing is different.
- Never cover any part of the face. Never overlap the captions. Never sit on a hand that is moving through that space if a small nudge avoids it.
- Keep it centred left to right.

Two earlier positions were tried and not kept: a title at chest height, and a title above the head on a wide opening shot.

## 4. Line breaks

This is the part the host cared about most.

> "In the right room is down on the line below because it just looks nice visually and it also breaks it up by keywords. Because I wouldn't have done 'why you need / to be in the right room'. It just wouldn't have felt as right... That just doesn't look nice to the eye and it doesn't read well either."

How to break a hook:

- **Break between phrases, never inside one.** Each line should mean something by itself. "in the right room" is a phrase. "to be in the" is not.
- **Keep the keyword phrase whole.** The words that carry the idea stay together on one line.
- **Balance the two lines.** Similar length reads best. A slightly shorter second line is fine. One stray word alone on a line is not, unless it is the punch ("AI makes good marketers / dangerous").
- **Two lines at most.**
- **Read it out loud.** The break should fall where you would naturally pause.

Breaks from an approved shortlist, as examples:

```
why you need to be            the hard part of AI
in the right room             just disappeared

AI is only as smart           the biggest lie
as you are                    people believe about AI

you need to                   firing your team for AI
progressive overload stress   is a mistake

"can't AI just do             1 strategist,
my ads?"                      10x the output

the only hire you need        the business you start
in the AI era                 isn't the one you keep
```

Breaks that would be wrong:

```
why you need                  AI is only as
to be in the right room       smart as you are

the hard part of              firing your team
AI just disappeared           for AI is a mistake
```

### One line

> "Some can just be all on one line. For example, the title hook, 5.9 million before 2 PM, something like that I would have all on one line. And then you'd probably need to just turn the scale down a little bit so it fits nicely."

- Short hooks that are one phrase stay on one line: "$5.9 million before 2pm", "the AI setup era is over", "why i quit my agency…".
- Turn the size down just enough that the box sits inside the frame with a clear margin on each side.
- If a line in a two-line hook is too wide, turn the size down a little for that hook too. Do not add a third line.

### How wide is too wide

The working rule: **the white box takes up no more than about three quarters of the frame's width.**

What that meant across 39 versions on the first job:

- Most hooks stayed at the normal size.
- One-line hooks of 21 to 24 characters fitted at the normal size (71 to 73% of the width). Only longer lines need turning down.
- Wider lines were turned down by up to about 15%.
- One hook could not be saved by size. "why i quit my marketing agency / for AI" filled 99% of the width. The break was moved to "why i quit / my marketing agency for AI", and the host was told and asked if they wanted their own break back.

So: turn the size down first. If a line would need to shrink more than about 15%, move the break instead, keep it on a phrase, and tell the user which hook and why. If it was their own line, the choice is theirs.

## 5. Put them on the clips

One finished version per shortlisted hook. Start from the clip's finished version with captions and music. Change neither. Name each file `ID_the-hook-in-kebab-case_title.mp4`. Keep a list as you go: clip, hook text with its break, file name.

Do the first one completely and compare a still with the reference (or the user's own example) before doing the rest.

Two routes:

**A. In Instagram Edits, driven by you (the default on a Mac),** or in the user's own editor. The route the first job used. Edits has Instagram's own Classic font, and the title stays a layer the user can change before posting. In Edits: duplicate the clip's music project, rename the copy "SOURCE ID title - the hook text", open it, add the text from 0 seconds for about 5 seconds in the Classic font with a white background pill, set the export settings again (they reset on open), and export to `~/Movies`. List the text tools first, since names can change between Edits versions. The cost is speed: each version is made by hand or one at a time, about a minute each. Tell the user the total up front. Add the text with the style above, from 0 seconds for about 5 seconds, and export at the clip's own size and frame rate.

**B. Burned in with the kit.** Faster, and it can run unattended. The title is baked into the picture, so it cannot be edited later, and the font is the caption font (a close match, not the platform's own). Early rounds on the first job were made this way, and the host preferred route A. Use B when the user has no editor or asks for speed.

How route B works:

1. Put the first hook in `clips.json` as `title_hook`, with `\n` at the line break.
2. Render with `--title` (`render.py --work $W --id 03 --final --title`). The kit finds the host's face across the opening shot, puts the box's top 50 px below the lowest chin, and writes `out/closeup/<name>.title.json` and `out/closeup/titled/<name>.mp4`. It warns if the box would not fit above the captions.
3. In `<name>.title.json`, set `frame_end` to about 5 seconds of frames (120 at 23.976 fps). The default ends with the first shot. Move `y_top` if the still shows the box too low or too high.
   To skip the re-render, write the file by hand instead: `{"text": "line one\nline two", "y_top": 1000, "frame_start": 0, "frame_end": 120, "style": "closeup"}` (pixels on the 1080x1920 frame; the box's top edge), then run step 4.
4. For each further hook of the same clip: change `text`, run `overlay.py --work $W --id 03 --title`, and copy `titled/<name>.mp4` to the version's file name before the next one overwrites it. This only re-runs the overlay, which takes seconds.

Music goes on the titled versions too. On route A, add the title to the version that already has music. On route B the kit works from the clean cut, which has no music, so mix the same song in afterwards with the same settings (see `music.md`).

## 6. Check every one by eye

For each version, pull a frame at about one second and look at it:

- Every word there and spelled right, including quote marks and "…".
- The break where it was meant to be.
- The box fully inside the frame, with a margin left and right.
- Nothing over the face. Nothing touching the captions.
- Captions and music still there. Same length as the version without a title.

`scripts/title_check.py TITLED.mp4 UNTITLED.mp4` pulls the frame, measures the white box (width, margins, centre height on screen) and compares the length with the version without the title. The numbers catch a box that runs off the edge. They do not replace looking.

## 7. Deliver

- Copy the checked files into a `With title hook` folder beside the others. Count them.
- Build one page of stills: one frame per version, grouped under a heading per clip, with the hook text under each. White background, lots of space, no long text. Stills are enough here, because the only thing that changed is the title.
- Send the page and the folder in one short message. Flag any hook where the break or position had to change. Small size changes do not need a flag.

## What to ask, and what not to

- Do not ask which hooks they want before writing the options. Write them and let them pick.
- Do not ask about style if they already have one or approved the one above.
- Do ask if a line they wrote themselves cannot be broken well in two lines, and offer the two best breaks.
